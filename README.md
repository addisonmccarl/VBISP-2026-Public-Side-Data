# VBISP-2026-Public-Side-Data

## What?
This is a repository for public side data work I did while at REU-PATHI in the summer of 2026. I used social determinants of health, namely  ADI and SVI, to predict block level crash rate and crash-level fatality rate (respectively). The primary regressions also involved 311 request data, daytime population, crash-level lighting, and road type. I experimented with incorporating speed limit data and more infrastructure variables (sidewalk quality, quantity, satellite lighting) but they were ultimately discarded because they were not useful and/or highly covariant. 

## Why? 
The pedestrian and bicyclist injury (and death) epidemic is an issue of public health. While literature on the topic has become more substantial, and awareness has arguably increased, [deaths continue to grow in America, while they shrink for other high-income countries](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40080454/). In practice, this means being a pedestrian (or bicyclist) in America is more dangerous than in other comparable countries. Many may attribute the epidemics exacerbation to the lack of pedestrian-centered infrastructure interventions in America, interventions that many other countries have taken. 

## Who? 
Like most matters of public health, some populations are disproportionately affected. [Those who are racially diverse, low income, or navigate a deprecated built environment experience disproportionate pedestrian injury](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/03611981231175888). One can imagine how these factors intersect: historic and systemic racism may segregate certain populations into low income neighborhoods, which in turn have a lack of pedestrian infrastructure (sidewalks, crosswalks, etc.), but also an increased number of people who rely on being a pedestrian for transportation, as they cannot afford a car. All of this creates a disproportionate risk for pedestrian injury. 

## So What? 
Local and state-level departments of transportation (DOT) have the ability to affect this issue through public policy. While it is largely known what must be done, data must be communicated to both citizens, government, and volunteers (such as those at [Vision Zero](https://visionzeronetwork.org/)) in order for officials to take action. This project, and the collation of its resulting findings, is one of many attempts to make these data communicable through visualization. Another, that is also local to Nashville, is [the Nashville Fatal Crash project](https://www.nashfatalcrash.com/).
